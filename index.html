BLACK PANTHER (2018)
Directed by Ryan Coogler
Screenplay by Ryan Coogler & Joe Robert Cole

A superhero film also tells us whose knowledge matters, whose culture belongs in the future, and whose decisions change the story.

My reading of Black Panther is that it challenges narrow stereotypes by imagining an African nation where cultural traditions and advanced technology coexist, and by giving Black women scientific authority, military leadership, and independent judgement. Its achievements are meaningful, even as the first film keeps its central struggle for the throne focused on men.

A note on the lens: representation & stereotypes ＋

Representation is how media construct an image of people, places or identities through choices such as casting, dialogue, costume and framing. A stereotype reduces a group to a fixed, simplified set of traits.

A film can reinforce an assumption, challenge it, or complicate it by revealing limits and contradictions. These are the questions guiding this study.

THE QUESTION

Can the future
look like home?

THE LENS

World-building
Costume & setting
Audience expectations

Wakanda’s first major reveal turns an assumption into a question. As the aircraft crosses its protective barrier, a hidden city of towers and advanced transport replaces the apparently ordinary landscape. The wide view gives the audience time to register the scale of a society built by its own people. By revealing the city from the arriving aircraft, the film makes discovery part of the viewing experience: the audience must revise what the landscape appeared to show. Against a stereotype that reduces Africa to poverty or dependence, Wakanda becomes a creator of knowledge and technology. The reversal matters because outsiders’ limited knowledge is exposed as a failure of perception. African people are positioned as the authors of their future. What stayed with me was the idea that advanced technology can come from anywhere. The reveal made me question why a futuristic African city should seem unexpected in the first place, and how familiar media images can limit what viewers imagine. [1]

The film also refuses to make progress depend on abandoning cultural identity. The Warrior Falls ceremony, with its varied clothing, jewellery and collective ritual, belongs to the same nation as Shuri’s laboratory. These settings are allowed to coexist. Costume designer Ruth E. Carter drew on multiple African cultural references, making Wakanda’s visual identity more specific than a generic futuristic city. [3] This combination suggests that tradition can help shape modernity. However, a fictional kingdom cannot stand for the experiences of an entire continent, and wealth should never be the condition for respecting a culture. What I found most striking was that Wakanda could be technologically advanced while keeping its cultural traditions visible. To me, progress does not have to make every place look the same. The film expands what audiences can imagine about African identity, without representing every African reality.

LOOK CLOSER What does the reveal ask the viewer to reconsider? ＋

The barrier separates what outsiders assume from what Wakandans know. The important change is in the viewer’s knowledge: the landscape was never the whole story. The film uses world-building to make the audience experience the limits of a stereotype.

REFLECTION TWO · GENDER & RELATIONSHIPS03 / 05
02

Women who
shape Wakanda.

KEY IDEA

Agency.

The ability to make choices and act on them.

Who decides?
Who creates?
Who changes the outcome?

Shuri challenges a gender stereotype through expertise. In her laboratory, she explains the updated Black Panther suit and demonstrates its ability to store and release kinetic energy. T’Challa, although king and hero, becomes the person who needs instruction. Their interaction gives a young Black woman intellectual authority: she understands the tools on which the superhero depends. Her earlier remark, “Just because something works, doesn’t mean that it cannot be improved,” also shows confidence in questioning an existing solution. [1] Her contribution helps create the conditions for his success. This is why I find her role significant: the film invites viewers to recognise scientific creativity as a form of heroism and to imagine who can occupy a field often stereotyped as masculine.

Okoye’s agency becomes especially clear when W’Kabi confronts her during the final battle. Asked whether she would kill him, she answers, “For Wakanda? Without question.” [1] W’Kabi then throws down his sword and kneels before her. This change in posture turns her words into a visible shift in power: her decision helps end their confrontation rather than remaining a statement of strength. Their relationship does not erase her public responsibilities or her judgement, and he is the one who must reconsider his actions. The scene challenges the expectation that a woman should give up her convictions to preserve a romantic relationship. Yet the film’s gender politics remain complicated: the central contest for the throne is between men, even while women provide essential knowledge, protection and leadership. The film therefore challenges traditional roles while keeping T’Challa’s kingship at the centre of its main story. Women’s influence on events matters, but so does whose leadership receives the central narrative focus.

Who has power matters.
Who gets to choose matters too.

A central distinction in this reading
LOOK CLOSER What separates strength from agency? ＋

A character can fight well and still have little control over the story. Agency asks whether the character has goals, makes decisions and affects outcomes. Shuri’s inventions and Okoye’s independent judgement make this distinction visible. W’Kabi’s surrender shows why the outcome matters: Okoye’s choice changes what another character does.

PAUSE & REFLECT04 / 05

The story continues
with the viewer.

Two questions to carry beyond the film.
Open each one for a possible starting point.

01Does a positive portrayal
automatically avoid stereotypes?↗

Admiration can still flatten a group into a single image. Wakanda opens up possibilities, but no single fictional society can represent every African experience. A useful next step is to seek a wider range of stories, including everyday lives beyond royalty and superheroes.

02Is being powerful the same
as having the freedom to choose?↗

Look at who sets goals, gives instructions and changes the outcome. Shuri’s technical authority and Okoye’s decision about W’Kabi show different forms of agency. Their influence also raises a further question: who holds the highest formal authority?

MY TAKEAWAY

Representation shapes the possibilities a story makes visible. My main takeaway from Black Panther is that advanced technology can emerge anywhere, and that a society can build its future without leaving its cultural identity behind. Shuri and Okoye also show why knowledge and independent judgement deserve recognition alongside physical strength. The film also leaves room to question who remains at the centre of power. That tension makes its representation worth discussing.
